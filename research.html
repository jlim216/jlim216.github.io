I study the international political economy of security, focusing on how interactions between states and private actors shape global interdependence. I am interested in how governments use economic statecraft and trade policy to influence commercial relationships, how global production networks affect interstate bargaining, and how domestic political interests shape foreign policy.

Methodologically, my research focuses on causal inference and computational methods for high-dimensional unstructured data. My recent projects include developing machine learning methods to discover latent concepts in text, images, and videos.

  • Sanctions Backfire? Corporate Political Response to National Security Restrictions in Geopolitical Rivalry

    Honors thesis for Stanford University’s Mathematical and Computational Science Program

    Abstract

    How do multinational corporations (MNCs) from geopolitical adversaries respond to national security sanctions imposed by host states? Existing literature focuses on the deterrent and constraining effects of sanctions but often overlooks how well-resourced MNCs strategically navigate such restrictions. Drawing on theories of corporate political activity, I argue that national security sanctions can intensify, rather than suppress, political engagement by targeted firms. When exit options are limited and core operations threatened, these companies adopt aggressive political strategies to safeguard their interests.

    To test this argument, I examine Huawei’s response to its 2019 designation on the U.S. Entity List using an original lobbying dataset from 2008 to 2022. Employing difference-in-differences estimation and Bayesian structural time-series modeling, my analysis reveals that sanctions by Washington significantly increased Huawei’s lobbying expenditure—contrary to the intended goal of limiting foreign influence. In particular, this surge was not driven by broader industry or China-wide trends, but reflects a firm-specific response to regulations by the host state. These findings challenge state-centric assumptions in the sanctions literature and highlight the political agency of well-resourced multinational corporations under geopolitical pressure. This study underscores the unintended consequences of security-driven sanctions and contributes to debates on economic statecraft and corporate agency.

  • Navigating Asymmetry: Leadership Preferences and Foreign Policy Outcomes in U.S. Security Allies

    Stanford honors thesis in International Security Studies (Center for International Security and Cooperation)

    • Co-recipient of the Annual Korean Studies Writing Prize, Stanford Center for East Asian Studies (2024)
    • Honorable Mention, Undergraduate Distinguished Essay Award, Hoover Institution (2024)
    Abstract

    How do leaders of allied states, dependent on the United States for security, shape foreign policy outcomes amidst pressures from the dominant ally and domestic actors? This thesis examines the impact of leadership preferences on foreign policy decision-making in South Korea and the Philippines, two key U.S. treaty allies in the Indo-Pacific. Drawing from interviews with over 40 foreign policy practitioners and detailed case studies, the research finds that allied state leaders’ preferences moderate the effects of both international demands and domestic opposition. While U.S. pressure and domestic opinion establish limits to policy choices, leaders have individual policy preferences that determine how they navigate these constraints.

    Under leaders who supported U.S. demands, the two nations strengthened their alliance with Washington. Conversely, those who favored a more independent foreign policy sought to balance U.S. interests with their own policy preference. In this process, leaders strategically aligned with domestic or U.S. demands, leveraging this alignment to justify their preferred policy stance and override opposition from the other side. Finally, although leaders skeptical of U.S. demands eventually made concessions to preserve the alliance, the degree of accommodation varied based on their stance. These findings highlight the relevance of individual leadership in asymmetrical security partnerships, offering insights into the interplay between structural forces and individual factors in alliance management.

Methodological

  • Outcome-guided Treatment Discovery with Unstructured Data (with Kosuke Imai)

    Presented at PolMeth 2026

    Abstract

    Researchers often seek to estimate the causal effects of attributes embedded in text and images before knowing which attributes constitute the relevant treatments. We develop an outcome-guided framework for discovering candidate treatments from high-dimensional data. Researchers first learn an outcome-relevant representation, decompose it with a sparse autoencoder, and retain stable, substantively interpretable features. Because outcome supervision precedes decomposition, the method allows researchers to discover latent attributes relevant to the research question. We then connect discovery to GenAI-Powered Inference, using training data to define candidate treatments and held-out data to estimate their effects. In applications to candidate biographies and Danish candidate photographs, the method independently discovers military-service content and an attractiveness-related image dimension and obtains positive causal estimates for both features, consistent with findings from prior studies. The framework helps researchers discover new treatments for causal analysis and future experiments.
